# The Last Ferry

[DM] I am a quiet dungeon master. Write your story here as long messages, and I will stay silent. When you want an answer, ask whether your character succeeds and I will consult the oracle. Sometimes the oracle refuses to decide, and then we roll for it.

You: The fog came down before the last crossing, the way it always does when someone on the far bank is lying about why they need to cross. I am only the apprentice. Master Wren is three days dead, and the ferry board, the pole, and the lantern are mine now whether the river likes it or not. The bell on the far bank rang twice: a passenger. Then, after a long pause, it rang once more, which is not a signal anyone has ever taught me.

You: I lit the lantern and poled out. Halfway across, the fog thinned just enough to show me the passenger standing at the water's edge in a ferryman's coat. It was a good coat. It looked exactly like Master Wren's coat, the one we buried him in. The figure raised one hand, not in greeting. In warning, I think. Or in recognition.

You: I keep poling toward the far bank instead of turning back.

DM: No, and something worse happens.

You: The pole struck something under the water that was not the riverbed. It struck back. The ferry slewed sideways, the lantern swung wild, and the figure on the bank lowered its hand, slowly, the way you lower a hand when the thing you were warning about has already happened. Cold water came over the board at my feet. Whatever was under us had hold of the ferry, and it was patient, and it was strong.

You: Check: Rivercraft (+2) on a d12, hard, target 9.

DM: Rolled 4 + 2 = 6 against target 9. Failure.

You: The pole tore out of my hands. Apprentice's hands, ferryman's river. I went to my knees in the wash and got both arms around the lantern post, because a ferry without a light is just a drowning waiting for its name.

You: I let the current take the ferry and ride the board like a raft toward my own bank.

DM: Yes, your character succeeds.

You: The current took the board and set me down, gentler than I deserved, in the reeds below my own landing. Behind me the ferry settled and went still, and across the water the figure in my master's coat was gone, if it had ever been a figure at all. The lantern never went out. I sat in the cold mud a long while, holding it like something alive. Tomorrow I will need a new pole. Tonight it is enough that the river gave one of us back.
